FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2 October 2026

Who comes
to Australia,
and why?

Millions of people fly in every year to holiday, see family, study or do business. This story follows where they come from, when they arrive, what they spend, and how tourism recovered after the border closed in 2020.

By Rayshav Thind
Data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics and Tourism Research Australia

1The Big Picture

How many visitors come, and what happened when the border closed

In 1991, visitors made 2.4 million trips to Australia. By 2019, that had almost quadrupled to 9.5 million.

Then COVID-19 hit. In 2021 just 246,240 visitors arrived all year. Even in 2025, arrivals were still below their 2019 peak.

35 years of arrivals

Arrivals grew steadily for three decades, peaking at 1.08 million in December 2019. Then the line falls almost straight to zero.

Each spike is a December. Hover over the line to see any month.

For most of the last 35 years, international tourism only moved in one direction. Annual visitor arrivals doubled from 2.4 million in 1991 to 4.9 million in 2000. Growth paused in the early 2000s, the years of the September 11 attacks and the SARS outbreak, and flattened again around the 2008 global financial crisis, but it never went backwards for long.

The 2010s were the boom years. Arrivals rose 61% in a decade, from 5.9 million in 2010 to a record 9.5 million in 2019. The rhythm of the line stayed the same throughout: a sharp peak every December, when summer holidays and Christmas bring visitors from around the world.

Then, in March 2020, the border closed and the line fell off a cliff. In 2021 just 246,240 visitors arrived all year, fewer than in a single quiet month before COVID. After reopening in February 2022, arrivals climbed back quickly. By 2025 they reached 8.9 million, or 94% of the 2019 record.

2Where Do They Come From?

Our closest neighbour, our biggest markets, and how the mix has changed

In 2025, New Zealand sent the most visitors: 1.44 million trips, about one in six of all arrivals.

Behind it, the mix keeps changing. COVID reshuffled who comes, and over 35 years the order of our biggest markets has been rewritten.

Where visitors live

Visitors come mostly from our own region. In 2025, 17 countries sent more than 100,000 visitors each, and most of them are in Asia and the Pacific.

Darker countries send more visitors. Drag the slider to see any year from 1991 to 2025.

The darkest shades cluster around us. New Zealand and China together made 28% of all visitor arrivals in 2025, and the ten biggest countries made 69%. Our nearest neighbours in Asia and the Pacific supply most of the rest.

Distance is not everything. The United Kingdom and the United States are on the other side of the world, yet each sent around 750,000 visitors in 2025. Many of these trips are likely visits to friends and relatives, through migration links that go back generations.

Europe as a whole is smaller than it looks on the map. The 16 European countries the ABS lists sent 1.58 million visitors between them in 2025, only slightly more than New Zealand alone. Drag the slider back through the years to watch Asia's shades darken.

Before and after COVID

The pandemic did not hit every route equally. Some markets came back bigger than before, while others are still well short of 2019.

Thicker lines mean more visitors. Switch between 2019 and 2025 to compare.

New Zealand's line is almost exactly the same width in both years, about 1.43 million trips. The United Kingdom has gone further, growing 6% past its pre-COVID level and moving up to third place.

China's route tells the opposite story. It was our busiest in 2019, but in 2025 it was still 28% smaller, enough to drop it to second place. Hong Kong was down 21% and Japan 15%. Malaysia fell 44% and left the top 10 altogether.

A few markets came back stronger. South Korea grew 42% and India 13%, and Indonesia joined the top 10. The recovery was not a return to the old normal: it changed who visits Australia.

35 years of reshuffling

Over 35 years, the order of the top 10 has been rewritten. Three countries, China, India and Japan, tell most of the story.

Each line is one of today's top 10. Hover over any line to follow that country.

China's climb is the steepest line on the chart. It ranked 21st in 1991, broke into the top five by 2005, and overtook New Zealand as our largest market in 2018 and 2019.

India took a slower but steadier path. It sat 26th in 1991 and has moved up in every decade since, reaching 5th in 2025 with 451,530 visitors.

Japan shows the reverse. It was our largest market in the early 1990s, peaking at 813,890 visitors in 1997, then slid to 7th. In 2025 it sent 423,120 visitors, fewer than in 1991.

3When Do They Come?

Holidays at home decide when visitors arrive here

December is our busiest month: 11.7% of a year's visitors arrive in it. January, straight after, is the quietest, with 7.1%.

But that is only the national average. Each country has its own season, and so does each part of Australia.

Every country has its own season

Visitors travel when they have time off at home. School holidays, festivals and northern winters give each country a different busiest month.

Read each circle like a clock face: January at the top, then clockwise. Bigger wedges mean a bigger share of that country's year. The sand wedge is its busiest month, and the dashed ring shows an average month.

The United Kingdom has the most lopsided clock. 17% of its visitors arrive in December, about one in six, escaping the northern winter and visiting family for Christmas. In June, just 3.7% come. The United States, India and South Korea also peak in December.

Asia runs on different calendars. China's busiest month is February, around Lunar New Year, with a second peak in July during Chinese summer holidays. Japan peaks in August, its own summer break, and again in March.

Singapore's peak is June, which lines up with its mid-year school holidays. New Zealand is the steadiest of all: no month brings more than 9.8% of its visitors or fewer than 6.2%, a sign of short, frequent trips across the Tasman.

Australia's own seasons

Inside Australia the seasons flip. The tropical north is busiest in winter, while the southern states fill up in summer.

Each row is one state, from north at the top to south at the bottom. Darker squares mean a bigger share of that state's visitors; the sand outline marks its busiest month.

The Northern Territory is the only state that peaks mid-year. July brings 13.3% of its visitors and the six months from May to October bring 60%, during the Top End's dry season, when the weather is mild and the roads are open.

Tasmania is its mirror image. December alone brings 14.6% of its visitors, and more than half arrive between November and March. NSW, Victoria, South Australia and Western Australia follow the same summer pattern, all peaking in December.

Queensland sits in between, with two seasons: a December peak of 10.7% and a second winter peak of 10.3% in July. The ACT is the odd one out, busiest in February, with smaller peaks in July and December.

4Why Do They Come?

The reason for a trip decides how long visitors stay and what they spend

In the year to June 2026, international visitors spent $40.5 billion in Australia over 8.4 million trips.

Most came for a holiday or to see family. But the biggest spenders were a much smaller group: international students.

Few trips, most of the money

Students make up a small share of visitors, but they bring in more money than any other group, holidaymakers included.

Each grid has 100 squares. On the left, one square is one trip in every hundred. On the right, one square is one dollar in every hundred spent. Sand squares are students.

Holidays are the most common reason to visit, at 44 of every 100 trips. Seeing friends and family comes next, at 33. Students make just 7, about 550,000 trips in a year.

Yet students spent $15.1 billion in Australia, more than holidaymakers' $13.4 billion, from fewer than one sixth as many trips. That is $37 of every $100 visitors spent here.

Visiting friends and family works the other way. A third of all trips, but only $13 in every $100: these visitors often stay with the people they came to see, so they pay little for a bed.

What one trip is worth

The reason is simple: students stay for months, not weeks. An average student trip is worth $27,374 to Australia.

Each bar is the average amount spent in Australia on one trip, for each main reason. Hover over a bar to see the number of trips and the average stay.

A student trip lasts 141 nights on average, nearly five months. Tuition, rent and living costs over a whole semester add up to more than seven holidays' worth of spending.

Visitors who come mainly to work are the only group that comes close. They stay 138 nights on average and spend $10,963 per trip, about three times a holiday.

Holiday, business and family trips are short, between 12 and 30 nights, and all are worth under $4,000. Family visits are the cheapest of all, at $1,873 a trip.

More visitors is not more money

New Zealand sends the most visitors. China sends fewer, but each one spends six times as much.

Further right means more trips; higher up means more spent per trip. Bigger bubbles mean longer stays, and colour shows the region. The trip scale is logarithmic, so each step doubles or more. Hover over a bubble for the full figures.

Chinese visitors spent $11.7 billion, 29% of all visitor spending in Australia and more than the UK, US and New Zealand combined. Their trips last 55 nights on average, more than four times a Kiwi's.

New Zealanders made 1.3 million trips, more than any other country, but most are short hops across the Tasman. At 12 nights and $1,802 a trip, they are 15% of trips but under 6% of spending.

Long stays, not big numbers, push a country up the chart. French visitors stay 84 nights, the longest of any country, and spend $6,811 a trip. Singaporeans and Americans stay 15 to 17 nights and spend less.

5Where Do They Go?

Which states visitors choose, and which have won them back since COVID

Most visitors stay on the east coast. In 2025, New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland together took 84% of all visitor arrivals.

But the recovery has not been shared evenly. Only one state now gets more visitors than it did before the pandemic.

Where visitors stay

New South Wales alone took 38% of arrivals in 2025, more than every state and territory outside the east coast combined.

Each circle sits in one state or territory. Bigger circles mean more visitors. Each label gives the state's arrivals in 2025 and its share of all arrivals.

The three east coast states dominate. New South Wales received 3.36 million visitor arrivals in 2025, Victoria 2.28 million and Queensland 1.84 million. These are the states with Australia's biggest international airports and its best-known cities.

Western Australia is a clear fourth, with 939,450 arrivals or about one in ten. Perth is only five hours' flight from Singapore, making it an easy first stop for visitors from South-East Asia.

Everywhere else is small by comparison. South Australia took 3%, and Tasmania, the ACT and the Northern Territory about 1% each. New South Wales received 55 times as many visitors as the Northern Territory.

Who got their visitors back?

Australia as a whole reached 94% of its 2019 visitor numbers in 2025. Only Western Australia has passed its pre-COVID level.

Every state starts at 100, its 2019 level. Lines that rise have gained visitors since then; lines that fall have lost them. Each state has its own colour, and the dashed line is Australia overall.

Western Australia is the only line that climbs. It received 106% of its 2019 visitors in 2025, about 55,000 more arrivals than before the pandemic.

New South Wales and South Australia are close behind, at 97% and 96%. Victoria, Queensland and the ACT are all stuck around 90%. Together, Victoria and Queensland received over 420,000 fewer arrivals than in 2019.

The Northern Territory has the furthest to go. At 79%, it received about 16,000 fewer visitors than in 2019, the biggest shortfall of any state or territory in relative terms.
